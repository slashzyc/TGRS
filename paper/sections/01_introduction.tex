\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{A}{ccess} to satellite observations is only the first requirement for a defensible benchmark. The selected observations must also support the locations, periods, and comparisons specified by the study. This requirement is easy to overlook when a public archive offers extensive coverage and a mature processing chain. Analysis-ready products reduce the effort needed to obtain consistent inputs, but they do not guarantee sufficient observations after a study applies its own spatial tolerances, quality rules, deduplication, and reporting groups. Reproducible remote-sensing research therefore requires an explicit account of how the available data support the intended analysis \cite{kedron2022}.

This paper examines that question for a coastal wind benchmark based on the Advanced Scatterometer (ASCAT). Coastal and high-resolution ASCAT processing has been evaluated in substantial product-development work, including the NOAA near-real-time coastal processor \cite{soisuvarn2023}. Such validation establishes what a product can measure under the conditions studied. It does not determine whether a particular subset of files contains enough observations near a selected set of buoy stations during a fixed period. Global coverage descriptions and station-level benchmark support address different questions.

We use \emph{benchmark readiness} to mean that a specified design contains enough eligible predictor observations to proceed to its planned outcome-based evaluation. Readiness is defined relative to a decision rule: the evaluation population, counting unit, thresholds, permitted repairs, and stopping condition must all be stated. A failed rule is informative only within that design. It does not, by itself, establish that the source archive is deficient or that a retrieval algorithm performs poorly.

The timing of this assessment also matters. Researchers may find that a benchmark has sparse stations or time blocks only after inspecting reference measurements. Replacing stations, changing the time window, or relaxing inclusion rules at that point can make the evaluation depend on the observed outcomes. Work on leakage in machine-learning-based science documents how evaluation choices can lead to overstated performance \cite{kapoor2023}. Prespecification separates planned analyses from decisions made after results are known \cite{nosek2018}. A predictor-only readiness check offers a practical way to identify inadequate support while reference outcomes remain unopened.

Our case began as a wind-performance study with eight evaluation stations, four development stations, five 28-day blocks, and a limited file-acquisition budget. The source protocol fixed 96 primary satellite files and four ordered rounds of eight alternate files. It required at least 400 evaluation opportunities overall and eight in each of the 40 station--block cells. If the primary files were insufficient, the protocol could activate only the next complete alternate round. It could not select individual replacements, change stations, or lower the thresholds. After all 128 files had been processed, both capacity criteria still failed, and the performance study stopped.

The present audit was formulated after that failure. It asks two questions: did the selected design satisfy the stated capacity rule, and can the recorded decision be reconstructed from the preserved predictor-side evidence? The distinction is central to the analysis. A later audit may verify that the capacity failure was recorded consistently; it cannot supply the missing observations or authorize the evaluation that the original rule prohibited.

The contribution is an implemented readiness procedure illustrated by a fully specified negative case. The procedure combines a unique station--observation counting unit, complete alternate-round activation, explicit final processing states, and a separate replay-integrity check. For the selected design, the available record reports 150 opportunities, a minimum cell count of zero, and only three cells meeting the threshold. The accompanying decomposition shows where the recorded shortfall occurred across stations, file strata, and acquisition stages. These are descriptive findings for one design. No buoy-wind error, model ranking, or retrieval-performance effect was evaluated.

The following sections place the procedure in the coastal ASCAT and research-provenance literature, describe the source protocol and later audit, and report the capacity and integrity results. The discussion considers what this case can inform about future benchmark design and what requires new data or independent verification.
