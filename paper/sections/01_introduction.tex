\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{A}{ccess} to satellite observations is only the first requirement for a defensible benchmark. The selected data must also support the locations, periods, and comparisons specified by the study. Analysis-ready products reduce preprocessing effort, but do not guarantee enough observations after spatial matching, quality rules, deduplication, and grouping. Reproducible remote-sensing research therefore requires an explicit account of how the available data support the intended analysis \cite{kedron2022}.

This paper examines that question for a coastal wind benchmark based on the Advanced Scatterometer (ASCAT). Coastal and high-resolution ASCAT products have undergone substantial validation, including the NOAA near-real-time coastal processor \cite{soisuvarn2023}. Such work establishes product performance under the conditions studied. The support available from a particular subset of files, near selected buoy stations and during a fixed period, remains a separate design question.

We use \emph{benchmark readiness} to mean that a specified design contains enough eligible predictor observations to proceed under its declared evaluation rule. The population, counting unit, thresholds, permitted repairs, and stopping condition must be explicit. A failure describes that design's support under those rules; it does not establish archive-wide scarcity or poor retrieval performance.

The timing of the check matters. If sparse stations or time blocks are discovered after inspecting reference measurements, changing the population or relaxing inclusion rules may make evaluation choices depend on the outcomes. Leakage in machine-learning-based science can lead to overstated performance \cite{kapoor2023}. Prespecification separates planned analyses from decisions made after results are known \cite{nosek2018}. A predictor-only check identifies inadequate support before the workflow authorizes reference-outcome access.

Our case began as a wind-performance study with eight evaluation stations, four development stations, and five 28-day blocks. Its protocol fixed 96 primary satellite files and four ordered rounds of eight alternate files. The capacity rule required at least 400 evaluation opportunities overall and eight in every station--block cell. An intermediate failure could activate only the next complete alternate round; it could not trigger individual file replacement, station substitution, or threshold relaxation. Both conditions remained unmet after all 128 files were processed, and the performance study stopped.

The subsequent audit asked whether the selected design met that rule and whether its recorded decision could be reconstructed from predictor-side evidence. The two questions yield distinct verdicts: integrity checks can reproduce a capacity failure without changing it. Because the audit was formulated after the failure was known, it remains exploratory.

The methodological focus is an explicit integration of a unique station--observation counting unit, a conjunctive total-and-cell gate, whole-round acquisition, final processing states, and a separate replay-integrity verdict. The reported case makes these decisions concrete: it contains 150 opportunities, a minimum cell count of zero, and only three cells reaching eight. Its decomposition locates the recorded shortfall across stations, file strata, and acquisition stages. This single-design evidence establishes neither a generally optimal selection method nor wind-retrieval performance; the original implementation and inputs remain necessary for independent reconstruction.
